# Content Standards

*Version 1.2, October 2026*


This document is the persistent constraint layer for all SEO content work. It applies at every stage of the workflow — planning, briefing, writing, editing, and reviewing. No skill output overrides these rules. When in doubt, these standards take precedence.

---

## Workflow gate rules

These gates are non-negotiable. They prevent workflow stages from collapsing when user messages are ambiguous.

- Do not produce a content brief until a SERP analysis and strategic angle have been presented and confirmed.
- Do not begin drafting until a content brief has been explicitly approved by the user.
- Do not begin editing until a complete draft exists in the conversation.
- Do not begin a final review until editing is complete.
- If a user jumps ahead (e.g. asks for a draft before approving a brief), flag this and return to the correct stage before proceeding.
- If no client context document or example-analyser output exists for the client, flag this before planning begins.

---

## Heading tag labelling

All headings in any content output — plans, briefs, drafts, and edited versions — must be labelled with their heading level tag before the heading text.

```
[H1] Title of the Article
[H2] Main Section Heading
[H3] Sub-section Heading
```

Never output a heading without its tag label. Never use a tag label without applying the correct heading formatting. This applies to every output at every workflow stage.

## Forbidden characters and constructions

### Em dashes

Em dashes (—) are forbidden in all content output. They are strongly associated with AI-generated writing and will be flagged by clients and editors.

Never use em dashes. Replace with:
- A rewritten sentence construction
- A colon where a list or elaboration follows
- A full stop where two clauses can stand separately
- A comma where the pause is light

This rule applies at the point of writing, not only at the editing stage. Do not produce a draft containing em dashes and rely on the editor skill to catch them.

### Forbidden phrases

Never use the following phrases or constructions in any content output:

- "in today's fast-paced world"
- "whether you're"
- "it's important to note"
- "delve into"
- "unlock"
- "elevate"
- "game-changing"
- "revolutionary"
- "seamless"
- "ever-evolving"
- "navigate" (used metaphorically)
- "boost"
- "powerful solution"
- "Additionally" / "Furthermore" / "Moreover" as paragraph openers
- "In conclusion" / "To summarise" as section closers
- "When it comes to"

### Forbidden cadence patterns

The following structural patterns signal AI-generated content to specialist audiences and must be avoided:

**Stacked short declaratives.** Three or more short sentences building toward a conclusion, each designed to land emotional weight rather than convey information. This is a marketing cadence borrowed from SaaS copy. It actively undermines credibility with professional audiences.

Bad example: "Maintenance gets deferred. Efficiency drops. The asset degrades."
Better: "Deferred maintenance degrades efficiency progressively — not dramatically at first, but expensively over time."

Wait — that example uses an em dash. Correct version: "Deferred maintenance degrades efficiency progressively. Not dramatically at first, but expensively over time."

**Rhetorical urgency performed at the reader.** Sentences that exist to make a specialist reader feel the weight of something they already know. This signals that the writer does not trust the reader's knowledge.

Test: if a sentence is trying to make the reader feel something rather than understand something, rewrite it so it does the latter.

## EEAT minimum requirements

These apply to all published content. They are not optional even for informational blogs.

**Link regulatory references.** Any named regulatory programme, government scheme, official standard, or industry body must be linked to its source. Do not name CHPQA, the Climate Change Levy, MCS, Ofgem, or any equivalent without a link to the relevant page. Use the GOV.UK or official body URL, not a secondary source.

**Anchor efficiency and performance claims.** Figures such as "80% efficiency" or "up to 40% cost savings" must be linked to a source (government guidance, published research, official industry body) or explicitly attributed ("according to GOV.UK guidance on CHP").

**No unsupported superlatives.** Claims using "most," "best," "highest," "leading," or "unrivalled" must be either sourced or removed. Generic authority language without evidence is an EEAT liability.

**Specificity over generality.** Concrete numbers, named frameworks, realistic operational detail, and accurate terminology carry more credibility than general statements. Prefer "typically every 1,000 to 2,000 running hours" over "regularly" or "frequently."

## Readability and scannability requirements

These are derived from the agency's readability training and apply to all published content.

**79% of web users scan rather than read.** Every article must support scanning. This means:

- Visual anchors (bold text, bullets, tables) must appear where content is genuinely list-like, comparative, or tiered
- No major section should be entirely solid prose if it contains parallel items, discrete options, or structured data
- Key terms should be bolded on first meaningful use within a section where a scanner needs a visual entry point

**Paragraph discipline.** One primary idea per paragraph. Paragraphs should ideally be 3–4 sentences. Large blocks of undifferentiated prose are a readability failure, not a thoroughness signal.

**When to use bullets.** Use bullets when content consists of discrete, parallel items of roughly equal weight that do not flow naturally as prose. Do not use bullets for content that is genuinely sequential or argumentative — that belongs in prose.

**When to use tables.** Use tables for comparative or tiered structured data: technology comparisons, maintenance schedules, service tiers, pricing structures. Tables should have a clear header row and meaningful column labels. Do not use tables for flowing prose broken into rows.

**When to use bold.** Bold key terms, lead-ins for bullet points, and critical figures or thresholds. Do not bold whole sentences or use bold for decorative emphasis.

**Sentence length.** Aim for an average of around 20 words per sentence. Vary sentence length naturally — some short for emphasis, some longer for explanation — but avoid sustained long-sentence blocks that make paragraphs feel dense.

## Tone standards

These apply across all content regardless of client or topic.

**Register must match audience.** For specialist, technical, or professional audiences (engineering, energy, healthcare, legal, financial): write as a peer with operational knowledge. Do not perform expertise — demonstrate it through specificity. Do not perform urgency at a reader who already understands the stakes.

**Confident, not salesy.** Content should feel informed and grounded. It should not feel like it is trying to sell the reader something or convince them that a problem matters.

**Editorial, not corporate.** The writing should feel like the work of a knowledgeable practitioner, not a marketing department. Avoid corporate jargon, inflated language, and generic motivational phrasing.

**Transitions must be invisible.** Transitions should emerge from the logic of the content. Do not use mechanical transitions ("Additionally," "Furthermore," "Moreover") as paragraph openers.

## SEO integration standards

**Keyword integration.** Primary keywords should appear naturally in the H1, within the first 100 words of body copy, and in at least one H2 heading. Secondary keywords should appear organically throughout without forcing. Never repeat a keyword awkwardly to hit a density target.

**Semantic breadth.** Strong SEO content uses entities, related terminology, and conceptually adjacent language naturally. This emerges from genuine depth of coverage, not from a list of keywords to insert.

**Internal linking.** Every published piece of content should include at least one internal link to a related article or service page where one exists. Placeholder links should be noted in drafts so they can be activated before publication.

**Meta elements.** Every content output must include a proposed title tag and meta description. Title tags should be under 60 characters where possible. Meta descriptions should be under 155 characters and read as a genuine summary, not a keyword-stuffed teaser.

## Distinctiveness and authenticity

These rules apply to every content type.

- **Never fabricate.** Do not invent client quotes, opinions, data, case studies, reviews, results, credentials or author details. Use a clear placeholder ([CLIENT INSIGHT: ...], [DATA NEEDED: ...]) and flag it instead.
- **Placeholders are decided at brief stage and never published.** The brief states whether the draft will contain placeholders, and approving the brief approves them. Every placeholder is listed at the top of the draft with an owner. A piece with any placeholder left in the copy is not ready to publish.
- **Plan distinctiveness in the brief.** Blogs, guides and articles must include at least one element only this client or this team could have published. Category, service and product pages should include one where possible. News and short updates are optional. See the Content QA Standard for the menu of options.
- **Real authors only.** Every byline is a real person or an honest team byline. Invented personas, AI-generated headshots and false credentials are deception and are never used.
- **People check the supporting details.** Titles, meta descriptions, alt text and structured data are reviewed by a person before publishing, the same as body copy.
- **Avoid template sameness.** Do not reuse one heading skeleton across many pages for the same client. Each structure should follow the topic and the reader.

## What this document is not

This document does not contain client-specific instructions, keyword strategies, or tonal preferences. Those belong in the client context document for each project. This document contains only the universal standards that apply to all content, all clients, all workflow stages.

When a rule in this document conflicts with a skill instruction, this document takes precedence.
When a rule in this document conflicts with a client's preference, flag the conflict to the user and resolve it explicitly rather than silently overriding either.
